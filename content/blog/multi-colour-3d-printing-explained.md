Multi-colour 3D printing lets us print a figurine with a red tie, a blue suit and black hair in one go, with no painting. It is how many of our busts and light-up letters are made.

## How it works

A 3D printer builds a part one thin layer at a time. In a multi-colour print, the printer holds several spools of filament. When the colour changes, it pulls one filament out, pushes the next one in, and cleans the nozzle so the old colour does not show.

## Why that adds to the price

- **Time.** Every colour change takes a short pause. A detailed figurine can have hundreds of changes, and they add up to hours.
- **Waste.** Cleaning the nozzle pushes out some plastic that cannot be used. It is thrown away, but it still has to be paid for.
- **Detail.** Small patches of colour spread through many layers cause more changes than a few large blocks of colour.

## What affects it most

1. **The number of colours.** More colours usually means more changes.
2. **How the colours are spread.** A part with a clean colour on the top half and another on the bottom half needs far fewer changes than a part with small colour details all through it.
3. **The size of the part.** A bigger part uses more plastic and more layers.

## A real example

A 4-colour figurine we priced recently needed about 189 g of filament and 12.9 hours of printing. Our estimator showed **LKR 9,600 to 14,600** for one piece. The slicer, which is the program that plans the print, later reported 172 g and 12.9 hours for the same job, so the estimate was close.

## Tips to keep the cost sensible

- Use fewer colours where you can.
- Keep colours in large areas, not small spots.
- Ask us if a one-colour print, painted later, would suit you better.
